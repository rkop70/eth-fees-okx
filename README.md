## What does “buy ETH” actually involve?

ETH is the native asset of the Ethereum network. When you buy ETH through an exchange, you are generally doing one of three things:

1. Purchasing ETH directly with fiat currency such as USD.
2. Buying another asset first, such as USDT or USDC, then exchanging it for ETH.
3. Trading an existing crypto balance for ETH on the spot market.

The first option is the most obvious for beginners. The second can be useful when a payment method supports stablecoins more directly than ETH. The third gives you more control but requires understanding trading pairs, order types, and fees.

The final amount of ETH you receive can differ from a simple multiplication of the ETH price by your dollar amount. The difference may come from:

- Trading fees
- Payment processing fees
- The spread in an instant-buy quote
- Price movement while the order is being confirmed
- A withdrawal or network fee if you send ETH elsewhere

OKX explains that its Simple Buy and Sell flow presents an all-in quote, while spot trading shows the market price and applies maker or taker fees separately. The spread is built into the quoted price on the simple route.

That distinction matters. An instant quote can be convenient, but “no separate trading fee” does not necessarily mean the transaction is cheaper. The relevant comparison is how much ETH arrives after every applicable cost.

### Spot trading

Spot trading is the more flexible route. Instead of accepting one instant quote, you choose an ETH trading pair and place an order against the live order book.

Depending on your account and region, you may see pairs such as ETH/USD, ETH/USDT, or another supported quote currency. The exact available pair should be checked inside your account because OKX states that product and pair availability can vary by jurisdiction.

The two basic order types are:

- **Market order:** attempts to execute immediately at the best available prices in the order book.
- **Limit order:** lets you specify the maximum price you are willing to pay. The order may remain open until the market reaches that price.

A market order is usually treated as a taker order because it removes liquidity from the order book. A limit order can be charged as either maker or taker depending on whether it rests on the book or matches immediately. The order type alone does not determine the final fee. Execution does.

For a beginner, the simplest approach is usually:

1. Deposit or purchase a supported quote currency.
2. Search for the ETH spot pair.
3. Choose a market order if immediate execution matters most.
4. Choose a limit order if you want a price ceiling.
5. Review the estimated ETH amount and fee.
6. Confirm the order.

A limit order is not automatically cheaper or better. If you place it close enough to the current asking price that it executes immediately, it may still be treated as a taker order.

## Should you buy ETH with a market order or limit order?

A market order prioritizes execution. It is useful when you want to buy immediately and the order is small relative to the market’s available liquidity.

A limit order prioritizes price control. It can help you avoid paying more than a specified price, but there is no guarantee that the order will fill. The market may move away before your price is reached.

A straightforward decision rule is:

- Choose a **market order** for a small purchase where immediate execution matters.
- Choose a **limit order** when you have a maximum price and can wait.
- Split a larger purchase into several orders if placing one large market order would move through multiple price levels.
- Check the estimated average fill price instead of looking only at the first price in the order book.

Limit orders are not a magic discount button. If the order matches immediately, it can be treated as a taker order and charged at the taker rate.
